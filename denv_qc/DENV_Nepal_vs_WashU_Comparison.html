Insert Size: The Defining Difference

Nepal Clinical

48bp
Median Insert Peak
  • Range: 38–88bp
  • Inserts far shorter than 2x231bp reads
  • ~180bp of each read is adapter
  • R1 & R2 almost entirely overlap
Cause: Degraded RNA from clinical plasma. Viral RNA in patient blood is highly fragmented.

WashU Cultured

211bp
Mean Insert Peak
  • DENV-1: 213bp, DENV-2: 252bp, DENV-3: 167bp
  • Inserts larger than read length (150bp)
  • R1 & R2 provide independent coverage
  • Minimal adapter contamination
Cause: Intact RNA from cultured virus. Cell culture provides high-concentration, non-degraded viral RNA.

Duplication: Opposite Pattern

Nepal Clinical

10.4%
Median Duplication
  • Range: 4.1–20.7%
  • Lower overall depth → fewer duplicate collisions
  • Typical for moderate-depth clinical sequencing

WashU Cultured

36.5%
Mean Duplication
  • DENV-1: 39.8%, DENV-2: 30.0%, DENV-3: 39.7%
  • Very high depth (1.1–1.4M reads) on 10.7kb genome
  • ~10,000x+ raw coverage → inevitable duplicates
  • Cultured virus = high viral titer = over-sequencing
Interpretation: WashU’s high duplication is a consequence of extremely deep sequencing of a small genome, not a library quality issue. After deduplication, effective coverage will still be ~6,000–9,000x.

Read Length & Effective Coverage

Nepal: 2x231bp

  • Nominal: 231bp per read
  • Effective mean: 84–144bp (after trim)
  • Much of each read is adapter due to short inserts
  • R1 and R2 heavily overlap → effective single-end
  • Unique information per pair: ~48bp (median insert)
Longer reads were wasted on adapter sequencing. A 2x150bp kit would have produced nearly identical usable data at lower cost.

WashU: 2x150bp

  • Nominal: 150bp per read
  • Effective mean: 150bp (full length retained)
  • Inserts > read length → minimal adapter
  • R1 and R2 provide largely independent coverage
  • Unique information per pair: 167–252bp
Read length well-matched to insert size. Efficient use of sequencing capacity.

Implications for Downstream Analysis

Consensus Assembly

  • Both datasets have sufficient depth for full-genome consensus
  • Nepal: short inserts may create coverage gaps at amplicon junctions
  • WashU: long inserts provide more even coverage across amplicons
  • WashU cultured samples serve as positive controls for validating the assembly pipeline

Variant Calling

  • Nepal: lower depth may limit minor variant detection (<5% frequency)
  • WashU: high depth (after dedup) enables confident variant calling
  • Nepal host contamination may produce false variants if reads aren’t filtered by MAPQ
  • WashU pure culture provides clean variant calls for comparison
Recommendation: Process WashU samples first through the variant calling pipeline as positive controls. Use the WashU consensus sequences as high-confidence references when evaluating Nepal clinical assemblies.

Conclusions

Both Datasets Are Usable

  • Q30 > 94% for all 43 samples
  • Sufficient depth for DENV genome assembly
  • Same primer set ensures compatibility
  • fastp trimming effectively cleans both datasets

Key Takeaways

  1. Insert size is the biggest quality differentiator — driven by RNA source
  2. R2 artifacts are run-specific, not protocol-specific
  3. Duplication reflects depth, not quality
  4. WashU samples are ideal positive controls
  5. Clinical samples need host read filtering that cultured samples do not

Methods

Nepal data: NCBI BioProject PRJNA1346681, 40 samples (DENV1: 6, DENV2: 30, DENV3: 4). Illumina MiSeq 2x231bp. Clinical plasma, PCR-amplified WGS.

WashU data: N997 run (Grubaugh), 3 samples (DENV1-3, 1 each). Illumina MiSeq 2x150bp. Cultured virus, same PCR primer set.

QC tool: fastp v1.0.1 with identical parameters: --qualified_quality_phred 20 --length_required 50 --cut_front --cut_tail --cut_window_size 4 --cut_mean_quality 20 --correction --overlap_len_require 30 --detect_adapter_for_pe

Aggregation: MultiQC v1.33
